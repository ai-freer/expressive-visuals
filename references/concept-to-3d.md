# 3D Reconstruction: Concept Art and Real-World Sources

## The target and its viewing conditions

First establish what the work is meant to match. An approved concept and style brief specify how the subject should read from the intended view. When the subject is a real object or place, search online for primary sources before fixing scale, geometry, or layout. Without concept art, those records can establish the physical target; choose a view that lets you check it. With both, use the records for the physical model and the approved image for the composition. When art direction changes, preserve the image decisions the revision leaves intact. Style names alone are too broad: silhouette, mark direction, edge quality, value grouping, and material response explain what makes this particular image distinctive.

Viewing conditions determine what a convincing reconstruction must preserve. A fixed illustration, an orbitable asset, and a playable scene expose different failures. A single image constrains visible relationships but does not establish hidden surfaces or depth uniquely. A concept may also bend perspective, proportion, or construction to serve composition. Such deviations can be effective in a fixed view; moving cameras expose their spatial costs. The useful choice is which image relationships to preserve, which spatial inconsistencies to resolve, and where the intended viewing range permits approximation.

Choose sources for the relationship you need to recover:

- **Consumer electronics.** Look for the manufacturer’s specification or dimensioned outline for the exact model and revision. For a phone, check whether the stated thickness includes the camera bump. Use a matching side drawing or profile photograph to place the bump; an overall thickness alone cannot tell you how far it projects.
- **Architecture.** For a historic pavilion, look for a measured plan and section in a conservation survey of that building and period. Read the column spacing in plan and trace the eave back to its support in section. A construction drawing records a design; if the target is the building as it stands, check it against an as-built survey or dated site photographs.
- **Geographic landmarks.** For a headland, use a mapping agency’s contours or elevation data for the relief, and dated aerial imagery for the shoreline. Before combining them, check their coordinate reference, elevation datum, date, and resolution. A terrain grid that resolves the ridge may say little about the narrow path on its crest.

Keep the source link and drawing, page, or dataset identifier beside the value it supports, with its units and what was measured. If credible sources disagree, check whether they describe the same variant, date, or measurement endpoints. Where the evidence runs out, leave the estimate adjustable. A comparable pavilion can suggest a joint but cannot supply a measured dimension for this one.

## Whole-image diagnosis and camera fit

When comparing with an image, match its framing, aspect ratio, and display size first. A known dimension anchors the model in world units; a camera fit determines how that length projects into the image. Small views reveal composition and value masses; target-size views reveal structural readability and surface marks. Across foreground, midground, and background, silhouettes, visible-face proportions, occlusion, supports, and negative space reveal whether an object fulfills its role in the image. More texture cannot repair a missing mass or misplaced dark opening; conversely, correct outlines with weak volume may implicate values, lighting, or atmosphere rather than geometry. The scale of the discrepancy helps identify which explanation is worth testing.

Use a grid or contour overlay to locate projected positions, spans, and gaps. Fractions of image width and height stay comparable across resolutions if the crop stays the same. Keep a few landmarks that describe both the intended change and a nearby relationship it could disturb: a roof peak to raise, for instance, and the opening between two columns that should stay clear. Save the marked reference and the camera and crop used for comparison. Where an edge dissolves into mist, mark the range you can actually see; leave a hidden edge unmeasured.

Patterns of landmark error help locate a cause, without proving it. Similar displacement across unrelated objects suggests a framing, camera, or overall placement issue; a discrepancy confined to one part suggests a local proportion, orientation, or structural issue. Reshaping each object to compensate for a shared error can damage an otherwise coherent scene. Horizon, vanishing directions, visible-face proportions, and known dimensions constrain camera height, orientation, field of view, and scene scale. Focal length changes framing, while camera position changes perspective. A useful camera fit explains several landmarks together; artistic perspective inconsistencies may prevent a single exact solution.

## Editable structure and visual readability

The pavilion at the end of a walkway looks smaller than the one at its entrance. Is it built smaller, or is it the same size and farther away? Both layouts can fit the roof outline from a fixed camera. Look for evidence the outline did not supply: a surveyed plan can locate the threshold; a person standing under the lintel gives a clue to its height. For a walkable scene, try the approach at eye level. If scaling down the entire pavilion leaves too little headroom, that rules out uniform scaling for this passage; a narrower pavilion with a full-height doorway is still plausible. Where the evidence leaves several layouts open, choose one that serves the intended view or walk and keep its dimensions adjustable.

Carry the chosen relationships across neighboring parts. Thickening a beam can close the clearance beneath the eaves even if its upper edge now matches the drawing. Check both the projected gap and the physical clearance in the working model. If a measured section shows a shallower eave than the artist drew, first check the camera. When the exaggeration is intentional, keep the approved silhouette and record the departure from the measured section.

Physical connection and visual readability are separate claims. Geometry inspection can establish contact; the target view establishes apparent scale, depth, and separation. A connected joint can disappear into shadow, while a projected overlap can conceal a disconnected support. For example, if a roof seems too heavy, a silhouette comparison can distinguish an oversized outline from a dark underside that merges with the supports; a neutral-material render then helps locate the contribution of surface color. Neither view alone establishes full visual fidelity.

If the roof needs reshaping, [Code-driven 3D](code-driven-3d.md) covers how to build and revise its form in code. If the roof already matches but viewers need to discover the joint beneath the eaves, [Code-driven 3D Interaction](code-driven-3d-interaction.md) helps design the action that reveals it.

## Competing mechanisms and regional treatment

A visible phenomenon does not specify its implementation. Geometry, surface color, lighting, and compositing can produce similar evidence in one frame. A watercolor bleed edge might be painted on a surface or generated at a light–shadow boundary. With camera and object fixed, moving the light separates these explanations: the painted edge stays put; the lighting-driven edge moves. The desired behavior under actual viewing conditions determines which route fits. When the concept cannot decide, the mechanism remains an assumption; equally suitable routes can be compared by cost and editability.

A coherent style need not apply the same treatment everywhere. Hard edges may explain construction, separated marks may articulate foliage, and broad value planes may carry rock volume. Masks can preserve these differences, but object and material labels are only proxies for visual function. Different regions of one object may need different edge behavior, mark density, or contrast to serve the same overall style.

## Comparisons that support causal judgment

A useful comparison addresses one causal question whose answer changes the next decision. Independent repairs can coexist in one iteration; changing several factors in the same comparison establishes only their combined effect. Naming the result that would disprove an explanation makes it possible to abandon a route instead of indefinitely attributing failure to unfinished tuning.

Controls depend on the uncertainty: comparing paint distribution benefits from a fixed model, camera, and light, while distinguishing color from relief may require a grazing view or light movement. Random arrangements are variables too. Stable samples in unchanged regions keep procedural texture or vegetation changes interpretable. If an edit has no visible effect, its contribution may be hidden by occlusion, fog, or compositing; isolation helps locate where the effect disappears.

Numerical comparisons support diagnosis but do not certify resemblance. Stronger gradients may indicate clearer form or more noise; matching value distributions says little about where light and dark occur. Corresponding regions need matching visual roles, not just matching pixel coordinates. Treatments also interact: local brightening and global grading can compound, while repeated softening can erase useful edges. Repeated compensating edits suggest an upstream cause or accumulated processing worth isolating.

## Integrated evidence and the next decision

Return to the saved view after the edit. Use the concept to judge whether the roof peak is closer to its intended position. Use a render from when the entrance read clearly to check what the edit disturbed: a post newly blocking the opening is a regression; an unchanged gap that was already too narrow is a remaining mismatch. An overlay often suffices. For repeated edits, it can help to flag changes automatically, but measure the relationship at risk: the two column positions can stay fixed while a post comes between them in the image.

Then remove the marks and look at the whole image at its intended size. The column spacing can measure correctly while shadow still swallows the entrance. Finer detail with the same dominant discrepancy suggests revisiting the diagnosis; a main view that holds until the viewer steps sideways suggests checking depth, occlusion, or geometry that only covers the original view.

Check the result against the claim: a matching skyline from one lookout does not establish that the ridge is at the right elevation, and a surveyed ridge height does not establish that the skyline reads correctly from that lookout. A local study tests a mechanism; a complete render reveals how geometry, materials, light, and atmosphere work together in that view. Neither a still frame nor an inventory of source files establishes editability or behavior in play; those depend on the working project. When concept pixels are sampled, projected, or extended, separating their contribution from independently modeled or generated content clarifies what the resemblance demonstrates and what remains untested.

Progress means that the original discrepancy has diminished without sacrificing established strengths. Remaining gaps clarify whether to retain, revise, or replace the approach.
