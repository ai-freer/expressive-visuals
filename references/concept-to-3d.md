# 3D Reconstruction from Concept Art

Start by opening the currently approved concept, any existing style definitions, and the latest actual output. Confirm which deliverable the user expects and the real viewing conditions. With a single image, start from the main camera; unseen backs and depth are inferences. A review request gets analysis only. When the user asks for a full-scene render, local experiments and untextured models are intermediate steps, not deliverables.

**Choose the problem from the whole image.** Match the concept and the current render in crop and display size. View them small first, then at the target viewing size. Ask: if surface texture were erased, what would still obviously look wrong? Walk foreground, midground, and background and check main silhouettes, the width ratios of visible faces, occlusion order, load-bearing connections, and places that should be empty. If the structure is unfamiliar, consult real examples or construction references to learn how its parts join. Even when a model with the right name exists, verify the role it plays in the frame. If silhouettes are right but volume or atmosphere is wrong, move to value, aerial perspective, and material; do not always rebuild geometry first.

**Turn visible relationships into editable constraints.** Use a few image landmarks, proportions, perspective cues, and occlusion boundaries to constrain placement; distinguish observed positions from assumed depth. Choose a representation that preserves connections, support, and shared paths as parts change. Derive related parts from shared guides where useful, rather than placing each independently. Check both their physical connection and their visibility from the target camera.

**Separate explanations before choosing an implementation.** Describe the visible phenomenon before deciding whether geometry, surface color, lighting, or a combination should produce it. If two routes both explain the main camera view, find a viewing condition where they predict different results. For example, a watercolor bleed edge can be painted onto the surface or generated along the light–shadow boundary: hold camera and object fixed and move the light. The first stays put; the second follows the shadow. Choose by the behavior the work needs. If one reference image cannot decide, record the choice as an assumption. When both satisfy a fixed-camera goal, take the cheaper one.

**Pursue one judgment per round, and only one that would change the next step.** Before acting, state: "I think the gap comes from X; holding these things fixed, I will look for this effect; if I see Y, I will switch to that route." Use the cheapest control that discriminates: swap materials with the camera fixed; use clay renders or silhouettes to separate modeling from lighting problems; hold model and light fixed to compare paint distribution; use grazing angles, light moves, or motion to separate surface color from real relief. Choose test conditions from the user's purpose; a fixed illustration camera does not need to survive a full orbit. Changing several factors at once only evaluates the combination; it does not show that any single factor works. If a change produces no visible result, verify that the changed element reaches the rendered frame. Isolate it or disable suspected occluders, fog, or compositing layers to locate where its effect disappears before tuning further.

**After review, keep, revise, or change route.** Put the sample back into the full image at its original scale. Recheck established landmarks, silhouettes, and connections after local changes, especially camera changes. Revert or revise a local gain that breaks the whole. If the detail got finer but the main gap in the full image remains, stop tuning and choose a new problem. If stroke direction is right but value blocks are wrong, change paint distribution or the painting method. If the first camera matches but a lateral move breaks it, examine occlusion, back-face coverage, or the chosen representation. When concept pixels are used, state what was sampled, projected, or extended and what was independently modeled or generated; one close approximation does not prove the method transfers. Fold the user's new corrections into the current iteration, and do not make the user judge every round.

**Return to the agreed deliverable.** A local study delivers concept crops, controlled comparisons, and the decision they support. Keep a repeatable way to produce matched full views and diagnostic crops, so each iteration can be checked against both the reference and the previous result. A full material render integrates the changes into the actual scene; open and inspect the final frame, and compare the whole image and the most diagnostic details. For an editable or playable version, verify in the corresponding project or real gameplay camera. Deliver viewable results, the needed source files, and the most obvious remaining gaps. If the full scene has not been rendered, continue the authorized work; stop only for a real blocker and state it. Do not substitute iteration counts, model lists, packed textures, or future plans for the rendered frame.

**To test whether the method transfers, run a trial without route hints.** Pick a concept the agent has not worked on, with different key visual traits. Provide only the goal, usage conditions, and necessary constraints. Before making anything, the agent names the most important trait and records candidate explanations with their differing predictions. Afterward, review three pieces of physical evidence together: a sample that captures the trait, a comparison that exposes the method's limits, and a keep/revise/change-route decision supported by that comparison. Judge whether the agent chose the right problem before user correction and made trade-offs from results; an original method that survives the comparison also counts as a valid judgment. If the user identified the key problem or technical route midway, record a guided trial. A single result describes performance on that case only.
